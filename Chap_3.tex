\chapter{Hadron reconstruction in ATLAS} \label{chap:Reco}

Before running the analysis, the signals recorded by the different ATLAS sub-detectors must be transformed into physical objects to deal with.
In this chapter, the reconstruction of jets is presented, since they are the main objects of interest for the analysis conducted in this thesis.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Tracks reconstruction in ID}\label{sec:Reco/Tracks}

The ATLAS detector relies on a complex magnetic system, as described in Sec. \ref{sec:LHCAtlas/ATLAS/magneticsystem}, to bend the trajectories of charged particles, allowing for the measurement of their momentum.
The starting point of these measurements is the reconstruction of charged particle trajectories, or \textit{tracks}, in the Inner Detector (ID). They can be identified by groups of three-dimensional spatial points known as \textit{hits}. 
Given the magnetic field configuration, tracks in the ID follow helicoidal trajectories, which can be described by the parameters: the transverse momentum $p_T$, the azimuthal angle $\phi$, the polar angle $\theta$, the impact parameter $d_0$, the longitudinal impact parameter $z_0$ and $q/p$, the momentum dividing the charge.
Those parameters are graphically represented in Fig. \ref{fig:Reco/Tracks/helix}.\\

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=2 in]{Images/Chap3/Helix_traj.pdf}
    \caption{Parameters describing a track in the ATLAS detector. Figure form Ref. \cite{ATLAS_tracking_update}.}
    \label{fig:Reco/Tracks/helix}
\end{figure}

\noindent The tracks are reconstructed using an \textit{inside-out tracking} strategy, starting from the innermost layers of the ID and moving outwards, with the requirement of at least 8 hits and $p_{\text{T}} > 500\text{ MeV}$ . 
The reconstruction algorithm begins by identifying clusters of hits in both the Pixel and SCT sub-detectors. 
Those clusters then form \textit{track seeds}, which are composed by triplets of them. 
Using a combinatorial Kalman filter, the track seeds are extended into full track candidates, on which a global $\chi^2$ minimization fit is performed to determine the initial trajectory parameters.
After adding compatible hits from the TRT sub-detector, if the requirements are satisfied, another fit with higher precision is performed to refine the track parameters.\\
The tracks which have been reconstructed as described above, are then stored and used to reconstruct the primary interaction vertex (PV).
Among all vertices, the one with the largest scalar sum of squared track transverse momenta, $\sum p_{\text{T}}^2$, is identified as the vertex of the hard scattering (HS) and its position is taken as reference point to reconstruct all the other objects.

\section{Jets reconstruction}\label{sec:Reco/Jets}

As described in Sec. \ref{sec:LHCAtlas/ppColl}, the scattering of two partons in a $pp$ collision produces high-energy quarks and gluons, which undergo a process of parton showering and hadronization, producing collimated sprays of particles, which are known as \textit{jets}.
A schematic of the jets nature is presented in Fig. \ref{fig:Reco/Jets/jet_schematic}.\\

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=2 in]{Images/Chap3/Tracks_cone.pdf}
    \caption{Drawing of a jet, showing the cone of particles produced by the hadronization of a parton.}
    \label{fig:Reco/Jets/jet_schematic}
\end{figure}

\noindent Jets costituents then form hadrons due to the QCD colour confinement, which are detected by the ATLAS sub-detectors.\\
The reconstruction of jets is a complex process, which requires the combination of information from different sub-detectors.
The diffent components of the spray are detected and reconstructed as different objects, which are then combined to form the final jet object.
The combination is the not trivial part and dedicated algorithms have been developed to perform it.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Jet constituents reconstruction}\label{sec:Reco/Jets/Components}
The reconstruction of jets starts with the reconstruction of their constituents, which are the physical objects detected by the ATLAS sub-detectors.
This process uses different algorithms depending on the target physics regime and kinematic regime, combining the measurements of the ID with those from calorimeters.
The common starting point is the clustering of calorimeter cells, which are grouped into \textit{topological clusters}.

%%%%
\subsection*{Topoclusters}\label{sec:Reco/Jets/Components/Topoclusters}
Topoclusters, or jet topological clusters, are three-dimensional clusters of energy deposits in calorimeter cells.
They  dynamically adapt their spatial volume based on the topology of the energy shower across both the LAr and Tile calorimeter layers.\\
The formation of topoclusters is controlled by the parameter $\zeta_{cell}$, the cell signal significance\cite{ATLAS_topoclusters}, computed as the ratio between the signal $E_{cell}$ and the average noise $\sigma_{noise,cell}$ in the cell:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Reco/Jets/Components/Topoclusters/zeta_cell}
\zeta_{cell} =  \frac{E_{cell}}{\sigma_{noise, cell}}
\end{equation}

\noindent All the cells with $\zeta_{cell} \ge 4$ are considered as seeds for the topocluster formation.
Starting from these seed cells, the cluster is grown by adding adjacent cells with lower significance, $ \zeta_{cell} \ge 2$. 
Finally, all the cells with $ \zeta_{cell} \ge 0$ are added to the cluster if they are adjacent to a cell already added to the cluster.\\
In the case of overlapping particle showers, the topocluster can contain multiple local energy maxima, which are then split into two separate clusters, with the requirement $E_{\text{cell}} > 500\text{ MeV}$.\\
To each topocluster is assigned a four-momentum $P_{\text{clus}}$ and it is treated as a pseudo-particle. In fact the mass is set to zero, since there is no information about the mass of the particles producing the energy deposits, and the energy is computed as the sum of the energies of the cells in the cluster:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Reco/Jets/Components/Topoclusters/Eclus}
E_{\text{clus}} = \sum_{i} E_{\text{cell}, i}
\end{equation}

\noindent The direction parameters of the cluster, $\eta_{clus}, \phi_{clus}$, are determined from the energy-weighted barycenter of the cluster relative to the nominal origin of the ATLAS coordinate system:
\begin{equation}
\eta_{\text{clus}} = \frac{\sum_i |E_{\text{cell}, i}| \eta_{\text{cell}, i}}{\sum_i |E_{\text{cell}, i}|}, \quad \quad \phi_{\text{clus}} = \frac{\sum_i |E_{\text{cell}, i}| \phi_{\text{cell}, i}}{\sum_i |E_{\text{cell}, i}|}
\end{equation}

\noindent Absolute cell energies are utilized in the denominator weighting to prevent directional bias from negative cell energy fluctuations induced by noise or pile-up subtraction.\\
Those kinematic variables are used to distinguish between electromagnetic and hadronic showers. 
In fact, as described in Sec. \ref{sec:LHCAtlas/ppColl/HadShow}, hadronic showers contain also EM components, resulting in the fact that the reconstructed clusters are only partially hadronic.
For this reason, after Local Cell Weighting (LCW) calibrations of the topoclusters, a probability $P_{\text{EM}}^{\text{clus}}$ is assigned.
It tells us the probabilty that the deposit is electromagnetic versus hadronic. 
Topoclusters with $P_{\text{EM}}^{\text{clus}} < 0.5$ are identified as hadronic, otherwise they are identified as electromagnetic.\\

%%%%
\subsection*{Particle Flow (PFlow)}\label{sec:Reco/Jets/Components/PFlow}

The Particle Flow (PFlow) algorithm \cite{ATLAS_PFlow} is built to optimize the reconstruction of jets with lower transverse momentum, $0.5\text{ GeV} \le p_{T} \le 40\text{ GeV}$.
This algorithm relies on the fact that at lower energies, the momentum resolution is higher than the energy one.
This feature is evident in the formulas associated with the two resolutions, Eq. \ref{eq:LHCAtlas/ATLAS/particleTracking/momentum_uncertainty_sagitta} and Eq. \ref{eq:LHCAtlas/ATLAS/Calo/ECal/HCal_resolution}, which show the different scalings with respect to the momentum ($\sigma_{p_{T}}/p_{T} \propto p_{T}$) and energy ($\sigma_E/E \propto 1/\sqrt{E}$) respectively.\\
The algorithm begins by the selection of the tracks to target: $0.5\text{ GeV} \le p_{T} \le 40\text{ GeV}$, $|\eta| < 2.5$, at least 9 silicon hits, zero pixel holes and $E_{clus} / p_{trk} > 0.1$ ($E_{clus}$ is the energy of the matched topocluster and $p_{trk}$ is the momentum of the track).
After this, PFlow subtracts from the matched topoclusters the expected energy deposit from charged hadrons of the selected tracks, replacing it with the expected energy deposit estimated from the track momentum $p_{trk}$, ensuring higher resolution.\\
If the residual energy of a topocluster after subtraction is consistent with fluctuations expected from a  single-particle signal, the remnant cluster is completely removed. 
If significant energy remains, it is treated as a neutral energy remnant.\\
The finalized PFlow constituents consist of selected charged tracks for charged hadrons and un-subtracted or remnant topoclusters for neutral hadrons and photons.

%%%%
\subsection*{Track Calo-Clusters (TCCs)}\label{sec:Reco/Jets/Components/TCC}

The angular separation between the products of hadronic decays is highly reduced in the case of highly boosted regimes.
The high track density and calorimeter energy overlap degrade the performance of the PFlow algorithm.
Track-CaloClusters (TCCs) offer a solution to this limitation \cite{ATLAS_TCC}.
They combine the track directional precision with topocluster energy measurements.
TCCs match the tracks, which are extrapolated from the primary vertex to calorimeters, with topoclusters if their relative angular separation satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Reco/Jets/Components/TCC/dR}
\Delta R < \sqrt{\sigma_{\text{clus}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{trk}}^2}
\end{equation}

\noindent In the equation above $\sigma_{clus}$ is the topocluster width and $\sigma_{trk}$ represents the track extrapolation uncertainty.
Unlike PFlow, TCCs do not perform energy subtraction; instead PU is removed.\\
The TCC objects preserve fine angular substructure within large-radius ($R = 1.0$) jets, improving jet mass and substructure observable resolutions ($\tau_{21}, D_2$).

%%%%
\subsection*{Unified Flow Objects (UFO)}\label{sec:Reco/Jets/Components/UFO}

While PFlow excels at low $p_T$ and TCCs dominate in highly boosted regimes, Unified Flow Objects (UFO) synthesize the two methodologies into a single unified input \cite{ATLAS_UFO}.\\
UFO are built by first executing the PFlow algorithm, where charged PFlow objects associated with pile-up interaction vertices are removed.
From the remaining neutral PFlow topoclusters uncorrelated soft radiation is removed.
The surviving neutral clusters and hard-scatter ID tracks that were not subtracted during the initial step are passed into the TCC cluster-splitting algorithm.\\
By combining PFlow subtraction at low $p_T$ with TCC angular splitting at high $p_T$, UFO inputs optimize small-radius ($R=0.4$) and large-radius ($R=1.0$) jet performance simultaneously across the full momentum spectrum.

\subsection{Constituents combination and jets formation}\label{sec:Reco/Jets/antikt}

After having reconstructed the constituents of the jets, they are combined to form the final jet object.
The combination is performed applying sequential recombination algorithms, which must satisfy two main requirements: \textit{infrared} and \textit{collinear safety}.
The first one, infrared safety, requires that the addition of a soft particle to the jet cluster does not impact on the jet itself.
The second one, collinear safety, requires jet invariance under the splitting of a constituent particle into two collinear particles.\\
The sequential recombination algorithms are based on the iterative combination of pairs of constituents ($i, j$), which are combined into a single object if their distance $d_{ij}$ is smaller than the distance $d_{iB}$ between each of them and the beam.
These distances are defined as:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:Reco/Jets/antikt/distanceantikT}
    d_{ij}=min\big(p^{2k}_{Ti},p^{2k}_{Tj}\big)\frac{\Delta R^2_{ij}}{R^2} \ \rm \rm, \quad \ \rm \rm d_{iB}=p^{2k}_{ti} 
\end{equation}

\noindent where R is the radius parameter of a jet and $p$ is an additional parameter about the power of energy.
$p$ could be equal to 1, falling in the $k_t$ algorithm, 0 for the Cambridge/Aachen and -1 in case of anti-$k_t$ algorithm \cite{antikT}.\\
For the purpose of this thesis, the anti-$k_t$ algorithm is used, since it produces regular cone-like jets around hard seeds and it is less sensitive to soft radiation.
Typically in ATLAS large-radius jets are reconstructed with a radius parameter of $R=1.0$ to collect energy deposits from boosted decays.
Small-radius jets, instead, are built with $R=0.4$ and are usually used to identify highly energetic partons generating a shower.\\
In Fig. \ref{fig:Reco/Jets/antikt/antikt} an example of anti-$k_t$ application with large-radius jet $R=1.0$ is shown in the case of a parton-level event associated with soft radiation.

\subsection{Jet calibration}\label{sec:Reco/Jets/JCal}

To ensure accurate measurements, a structured calibration workflow is implemented on the reconstructed jets.
In the first place, a correction due to the presence of pile-up is applied. It consists in the subtraction to the jet energy, based on the average energy contribution from PU ($\rho$) and the jet area.
This correction is applied making use of MC simulations.\\
The second step is the Jet Energy Scale (JES) calibration, which accounts for detector response and resolution effects, as well as for the presence of inactive material in the calorimeter.
JES is applied matching the reconstructed jets with the corresponding truth jets in MC simulations, and it is derived as a function of the jet $p_T$ and $\eta$.\\
Another calibration, known as Global Sequential Calibration (GSC), is applied via sequential multiplicative corrections, to absorb residual differences in the ATLAS response due to variations in the energy distributions of constituents or the jet flavor and topology.
GSC is derived from energy measurements in certain sections of the experimental apparatus.\\
Finally, an \textit{in-situ} calibration is applied to correct for residual differences between data and MC simulation, using well-measured reference objects such as photons or $Z$ bosons.\\
Associated to each approach cited above, a systematic uncertainty is derived, which is then propagated to the final analysis.
At low $p_T$ the dominant uncertainty is due to PU and different detector response; JES, which is below 5\%, is the main source of uncertainty at high momentum ($p_T > 400\text{ GeV}$).\\
The JER, jet energy resolution, can be espressed, as a function of $p_T$ in a form similar to those for the energy resolution of the calorimeters:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Reco/Jets/JCal/JER}
\frac{\sigma_{p_T}}{p_T} =  \frac{S}{\sqrt{p_T}} \oplus \frac{N}{p_T} \oplus C
\end{equation}

\noindent with S the stochastic term associated with detector response, N the noise term and C the constant term associated with jet formation, which is the major contribution at high momentum.

\noindent Subtructure can then discriminate between jets originating from different physical processes, such as the decay of the top quark in three "prongs"\footnotemark, the decay of a $W/Z$ boson in two "prongs" or a single-prong QCD or BSM jet.
Specific variables, based on the kinematic properties of the jet constituents and their spatial distribution within the jet, have been developed to allow this classification.
Those variables presented below are used in the analysis conducted in this thesis, as comparison with the results obtained from the Anomaly Detection approach.

\footnotetext{\textit{Prong} is the colloquial term used to refer to the constituents of the jet substructure.}

%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{$N$-Subjettiness ($\tau_N$)} \label{sec:Reco/Vars/tau_N}
The $N$-subjettiness \cite{tau_N}, denoted as $\tau_N$, is used to quantify the compatibility of a jet reconstruction with an $N$-prong hypothesis.
Following the jet reconstruction as described in the previous section, $\tau_N$ is calculated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Reco/Vars/tau_N}
\tau_N = \frac{1}{R_0 \sum_i p_{\text{T},i}} \sum_i p_{\text{T},i} \min \left\{ \Delta R_{1,i}, \Delta R_{2,i}, \dots, \Delta R_{N,i} \right\}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $i$ runs over all jet constituents, $R_0$ is the jet radius and $\Delta R_{k,i}$ represents the distance between constituent $i$ and subjet axis $k$.
Small values of $\tau_N$ indicate that the radiation is aligned with the direction of the $N$ subjects, therefore the jet is compatible with an $N$-prong hypothesis, while large values of $\tau_N$ indicate that the jet are compatible with the $N+1$-prong and higher hypothesis.
The ratio of two subjettiness is usually preferred to discriminate between different prong hypotheses. It is defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Reco/Vars/tau_ratio}
\tau_{N, N-1} = \frac{\tau_N}{\tau_{N-1}}
\end{equation}
The ratio $\tau_{21} = \tau_2 / \tau_1$ can isolate two-prong vector boson decays such as $W/Z$ from single-prong QCD jets. 
$\tau_{32} = \tau_3 / \tau_2$, instead, can be used to tag three-prong top quark decays.\\
In Fig. \ref{fig:Reco/Vars/tauN} example distributions of $\tau_2$ and $\tau_{21}$ are shown for jets produced in $W$ and QCD processes. 
It shows that the two-prong hypothesis is more compatible with the former than the latter and this can be identified more easily using the ratio $\tau_{21}$.

\subsection{Energy Correlation Functions (ECFs) and $D_2$}\label{sec:Reco/Vars/D2}
Energy Correlation Functions (ECFs) \cite{ECF,D2} are used, once again, to tag jets with substructure without necessitating the definition of candidate subjet axis.
Referred to the jet $J$, they are defined as:
\begin{align}\label{eq:Reco/Vars/D2/ECF}
\text{ECF}_0 &= 1 , \quad \text{ECF}_1 = \sum_{i \in J} p_{\text{T},i} \\
\text{ECF}_2(\beta) &= \sum_{i<j \in J} p_{\text{T},i} p_{\text{T},j} (\Delta R_{ij})^\beta \\
\text{ECF}_3(\beta) &= \sum_{i<j<k \in J} p_{\text{T},i} p_{\text{T},j} p_{\text{T},k} (\Delta R_{ij} \Delta R_{ik} \Delta R_{jk})^\beta
\end{align}
where $\beta$, usually set to 1, is an angular weighting exponent. 
A combination of these function is used to define $D_2$, which is a powerful discriminant to identify two-prong substructure within jets.
The $D_2$ variable is derived as:
\begin{equation}
D_2 = \frac{\text{ECF}_3 \cdot (\text{ECF}_1)^3}{(\text{ECF}_2)^3}
\end{equation}
\noindent Similarly to $\tau_2$, lower values of $D_2$ are associated with a higher probability of the jet being produced by a two-prong decay, while higher values indicate more probable single-prong origin.
An example of the $D_2$ distributions for jets produced by $Z$ and QCD processes is shown in Fig. \ref{fig:Reco/Vars/D2/D2}.
